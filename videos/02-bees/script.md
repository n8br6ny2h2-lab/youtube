4. **Nasıl inşa ediyorlar?** Karınlarındaki bezlerden balmumu pulları çıkar. Bilim insanları hâlâ tartışıyor: hücreler daire başlayıp ısıyla mı altıgene dönüşüyor, yoksa arılar mı şekil veriyor?

6. **Bal nasıl yapılır?** Nektar çok sulu; arılar enzim ekler, kanat çırparak suyu buharlaştırır, balmumuyla kapatır. 450 g bal için ~2 milyon çiçek.

## 0:00 Hook
[SAHNE: 4K makro: petek hücrelerinin üzerinde yürüyen arılar. Kamera yavaşça yaklaşır; altıgenlerin üzerine ince altın çizgiler çizilir.]

This is one of the most perfect structures in nature. Thousands of six-sided cells, almost identical, built in the dark, by insects with brains smaller than a grain of rice.

Bees have been building it for millions of years. But mathematicians could only prove why it works in 1999.

So how do bees do it? And why hexagons, and not squares or circles? Let's go inside the hive.

[SAHNE: Kavci Lab logo, 2 saniye.]

## 0:40 Inside the hive
[SAHNE: Kovan girişinde uçuşan arılar (4K). Sonra çerçeveyi kaldıran arıcı.]

A honey bee colony is like a city. In summer, it can hold tens of thousands of bees. But only one of them is the queen. Her job is to lay eggs, up to around two thousand in a single day.

[SAHNE: 2D: kraliçe, işçi, erkek arı ikonları; sayılar.]

Almost all the other bees are workers, and they're all female. They clean, build, feed the young, guard the door, and fly out for food. The few males, called drones, have one job: to mate with a queen from another colony.

A summer worker lives only about five or six weeks. And she spends almost all of it working.

## 1:40 The hexagon puzzle
[SAHNE: 2D: aynı alanı kaplayan daireler (aralarında boşluk), kareler, üçgenler ve altıgenler yan yana.]

To store honey and raise young, bees need lots of small containers. And there's a problem: the walls are made of wax, and wax is expensive. To make it, bees eat a lot of honey. Roughly six to eight kilograms of honey for every kilogram of wax.

So the challenge is this: how do you build as many cells as possible, using as little wax as possible?

Circles don't work well. They leave gaps between them, so space is wasted. Only three regular shapes fit together with no gaps at all: triangles, squares, and hexagons.

[SAHNE: 2D: her şeklin çevresi sayaçla ölçülür; altıgen en kısa çevreyle kazanır.]

And of those three, the hexagon needs the shortest walls to hold the same amount of space. Less wall means less wax, and less wax means more honey saved.

People suspected this for about two thousand years. It was called the honeycomb conjecture. In 1999, the mathematician Thomas Hales finally proved it: if you want to divide a flat surface into equal areas with the least total wall length, hexagons are the best possible answer.

Bees got there first.

## 3:00 How do they build it?
[SAHNE: 4K makro: petek yapan arılar, taze beyaz balmumu.]

Young worker bees make the wax inside their own bodies. They have special glands on the underside of their abdomen. The wax comes out as tiny, clear flakes.

[SAHNE: 2D: arının karın altında küçük balmumu pulları.]

The bees chew these flakes to soften them, then press them into place, building the walls about as thin as a sheet of paper, with remarkable precision.

But how do they get such perfect hexagons? Here, scientists still disagree.

One idea is that bees build roughly round cells, and the warm wax, heated by the bees' bodies, slowly flows and pulls into hexagons, a bit like soap bubbles pressing together. Other researchers have found evidence that the bees actively shape the angles themselves. It may be a mix of both. It's still an open question.

## 4:00 The waggle dance
[SAHNE: Çiçekten çiçeğe uçan arı (4K). Sonra petek üzerinde kalabalık arılar.]

Building is only half the story. A colony also has to find food, sometimes kilometers away. So how does one bee tell the others where the flowers are?

She dances.

[SAHNE: 2D: petek üzerinde sekiz şeklinde dans yolu; ortadaki düz "sallanma" kısmı vurgulanır.]

Back inside the dark hive, a forager runs in a figure-eight pattern on the honeycomb. In the middle of each loop, she runs in a straight line while shaking her body from side to side. This is called the waggle dance.

[SAHNE: 2D: dikey çizgi = Güneş yönü; sallanma açısı = çiçeğin yönü. Yan panelde gökyüzü haritası.]

The direction of that straight run tells the others where to fly. If she waggles straight up, it means: fly toward the Sun. If she waggles to the right of straight up, the flowers are that same angle to the right of the Sun.

And the length of the waggle tells the distance. The longer she waggles, the farther away the food.

The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch decoded this language, and in 1973, he shared the Nobel Prize for it.

## 5:20 Making honey
[SAHNE: Çiçekte nektar toplayan arı (4K makro).]

Now, about the honey itself. Bees don't collect honey. They collect nectar, a sugary liquid from flowers. And nectar is mostly water.

Back at the hive, bees pass the nectar from mouth to mouth and add enzymes that break the sugars down. Then they put it into the cells and fan their wings over it, day and night, to evaporate the water.

[SAHNE: 4K: kanat çırpan arılar. 2D: su oranı göstergesi; nektar → bal, su oranı düşer.]

When the honey is thick enough, with only about seventeen percent water left, they seal each cell with a thin cap of wax. Like that, it can last for a very long time.

To make just one jar, about half a kilogram, bees have to visit around two million flowers.

## 6:20 Air conditioning
[SAHNE: Kovan girişinde kanatlarını çırparak hava akımı yapan arılar.]

There's one more trick. The center of the hive, where the young bees grow, is kept at about thirty-five degrees Celsius, almost like a warm body.

When it's cold, bees shiver their flight muscles to make heat. When it's hot, they line up and fan their wings to push air through the hive, and some even bring water to cool it down, like a natural air conditioner.

A whole city, with its own architecture, language, food factory, and climate control. All built by tiny insects.

## 7:00 Why it matters
[SAHNE: Meyve bahçesi, çiçekli tarla, sebze ve meyveler (4K).]

And this matters to us too. As bees fly from flower to flower, they carry pollen, and that helps many plants make fruits and seeds. According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, about one in every three bites of food we eat depends on pollinators like bees.

So the next time you see a bee, remember: it might be on its way home to a perfect hexagon, ready to dance.
